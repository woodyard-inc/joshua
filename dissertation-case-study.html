Case Study · 002 · 2024 · Social Capital & Life Satisfaction

Money can't buy happiness— but community can

InstitutionCity St George's, University of London ProgrammeMSc Behavioural Economics RoleLead researcher & author Duration~6 months · 2024 SupervisorDr Anke Plagnol ResultDissertation mark: 71% · with Merit
In short

Social Capital & Life Satisfaction. A cross-cultural analysis of 11,249 respondents across Austria, China, Russia, South Africa and the United States, using the ISSP 2017 module as a single instrument for all three of Putnam's social capital dimensions.

Does social capital make lives better everywhere, or does it depend on where you live?

Finding 01

Social capital is positively associated with life satisfaction across all five countries, but the relationship is not uniform. It is shaped by culture, institutions and environment.

Finding 02

Perceived institutional fairness was the most consistent cross-cultural predictor of life satisfaction, while relative income had no significant main effect.

Finding 03

Social capital carries more of the weight where the state does less: its associations weakened as human development, government resources and governance quality rose. Trust and fairness, however, remained consistently associated.

Context § 01

Does social capital make lives better everywhere, or does it depend on where you live? I tested it across five countries: Austria, China, Russia, South Africa and the United States.

Submitted for the MSc Behavioural Economics, City St George's, University of London. Dissertation mark: 71%, with Merit.

Overall, this is a very comprehensive study of the relationship between life satisfaction and social capital in various national contexts. It was a pleasure to read this thoughtful analysis.

— Dr Anke Plagnol · supervisor

Problem § 02

Social capital research often treats its link with life satisfaction as uniform, and measures its dimensions with separate instruments.

My contribution was to use the underused ISSP 2017 module as a single instrument measuring all three of Putnam's social capital dimensions at once. National indices then served as moderators: human development, government resources, corruption, natural disaster risk, Hofstede's cultural values and relative income.

This gives a point of empirical comparison across national contexts, and it is the most portable part of the work.

Approach & methods § 03

  • DataISSP 2017, n = 11,249, analysed in R.
  • H1aSocial capital and life satisfaction.
  • H1bDifferences across countries.
  • H2aImplicit circumstances: natural disaster risk and culture.
  • H2bExplicit national circumstances: human development, government resources, corruption and income.
  • MethodsMultiple imputation (MICE, predictive mean matching, grouped by country); PCA for multi-item constructs; country-level and pooled OLS with interaction terms; LSmeans with Tukey pairwise comparisons; VIF multicollinearity checks; AIC/BIC for comparing moderator models.

Life satisfaction was the ISSP's single 7-point item. Social capital was measured through nine constructs covering trust, social networks, support resources, norms of reciprocity and contact frequency, with multi-item constructs reduced by PCA (most α ≥ 0.71). Controls were age, partner status, ISCO-08 skill level and household financial ease.

Results § 04

The data are cross-sectional, so every result below is an association, not an effect.

Overall — H1a

Social capital was positively associated with life satisfaction, extending Putnam (2000) internationally: support and trust are linked to better lives.

The surprise: organisational support resources were negatively associated with life satisfaction, suggesting citizens may dislike relying on government for resources.

By country — H1b

  • TrustTrust measures were significant in almost all countries; social networks in South Africa; societal norms and resources in China.
  • FairnessFairness was a fundamental variable in every country, and Austria's coefficient was almost double the others'. Austria's trust in fairness and organisational trust differed significantly from the rest of the sample, consistent with the “warm glow” of trust in highly developed countries (Bjørnskov, 2003b).
  • Russia, South AfricaBoth still working towards the highest levels of human development, they showed a particular dependence on trust in society. Stable social relations may matter more under geopolitical uncertainty.
  • ChinaPositive associations with organisational support resources and societal care norms, reflecting a cultural value placed on cohesion and harmony.
  • TukeyAt average social capital, every country had significantly different life satisfaction. China was lowest, which may be explained by guanxi (關係), where networks also carry economic opportunity (He & Gebhardt, 2015).

Moderation — H2a and H2b

Table 01How national circumstances changed the link between social capital and life satisfaction
ModeratorWhat changedInterpretation
Natural disaster risk The positive associations of trust and social networks weakened in riskier areas, while societal care strengthened. Natural risk had no positive significant association with life satisfaction. Preventive infrastructure alone does not improve lives; community development does (Aldrich & Meyer, 2014; Sato et al., 2020; Toya & Skidmore, 2014).
Power distance Higher power distance strengthened the association of organisational support resources and weakened that of social networks. Power distance itself was negatively associated with life satisfaction. Hierarchy increases reliance on institutions and limits network interaction.
Masculinity As masculinity rose, the association of trust in society weakened and trust in fairness strengthened. The elder care norm mattered more in feminine cultures. Competitive cultures lean on fair systems to keep cohesion.
Individualism vs collectivism Social network value mattered more in individualistic cultures. Societal care, organisational contact and organisational support mattered more in collectivist cultures. Bridging social capital in individualistic cultures (Putnam, 2000); in-group expectations of care in collectivist ones.
Human development, government resources, lower corruption Each weakened the positive association between social capital and life satisfaction, while governance quality had its own significant positive main effect (less corruption: β = .167, p < .001). As perceived corruption fell, the associations weakened for trust in society (β = −.019, p < .05), social networks (β = −.019, p < .01), organisational support resources (β = −.023, p < .001) and organisational contact (β = −.013, p < .01). When the state provides better resources, citizens are less burdened and draw life satisfaction from a wider range of sources (Bjørnskov, 2003a; Lambsdorff, 2003; Özcan & Bjørnskov, 2011).
Relative income (within-country brackets) No significant main effect. As income rose, the associations weakened for trust in society (β = −.014, p < .01), daily contact (β = −.001, p < .001) and organisational contact (β = −.013, p < .01), while societal care strengthened (β = .016, p < .001). Consistent with Easterlin (2015). Higher earners may be more conservative in their social relations, or may draw utility from pro-sociality rather than income (Aknin et al., 2013).

One caveat on income: household financial ease, a control for making ends meet, was one of the strongest predictors throughout. Relative income did not predict satisfaction, but felt financial strain did.

Implications § 05

The relationship between social capital and life satisfaction depends on context, so culture and existing institutions need to be analysed before any intervention. Most social capital associations weaken as state capacity grows, but trust and fairness stay consistently linked to life satisfaction, and income shows no significant effect. In developed societies, strategies that build community trust and fair institutions may achieve more than economic promises.

Recommendations

  • High disaster riskCommunity emergency action plans and regular community events. These strengthen trust and organisational support, protect citizens in a disaster, and cost little compared with large-scale infrastructure.
  • All contextsEspecially in advanced democracies, prioritise justice (even for petty crimes), fair wage distribution and social benefits, through transparent institutions, expanded social resource services and progressive taxation. Where trust in institutions and in each other erodes, life satisfaction falls and civil unrest becomes more likely.
  • Individualistic culturesSubsidise or create “network drivers” that build bridging ties, informal (parks, pubs) and formal (employment resource centres, national sporting events), and teach tolerance and inclusivity from a young age.

Theoretical implication: significant interactions suggest limits to what institutions alone can do for wellbeing, because culture and physical environment shape how citizens interact.

What I'd do next § 06

  • Wider sampleFive countries is diverse but not globally representative. A wider sample would test generalisability and give a measured point of comparison for adapting development ideas across cultures.
  • PersonalityPersonal characteristics and personality traits may mediate or moderate the relationship; a dedicated dataset could test this. Tulin, Lancee and Volker (2018) show personality traits predict forms of social capital, and the ISSP doesn't measure them.
  • Better normsNorms need better proxies. A dedicated questionnaire polling a truly worldwide sample would help.
  • DesignA longitudinal panel, even two waves, would separate “social capital leads to satisfaction” from “satisfied people build social capital”.
  • Future researchThe multidimensional nature of social capital, its long-term consequences, which promotion strategies work, and the critical circumstances where it matters most.

Better social capital will lead to better lives.
