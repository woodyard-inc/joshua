In short

A sales-data investigation across eight NY metro stores. During the summer sale, around a dozen top-50 products were bought together (0.90+ correlation), yet prime floor space went elsewhere. The recommendation: put the products shoppers were choosing where they would see them first.

The sales data couldn't tell me who was buying. But it could tell me what was being bought together — and on a hot Saturday in SOHO, the floor told me the rest.

Context § 01

I was a part-time freelance researcher embedded with UNIQLO's Global Creative Lab, running open-brief qualitative and quantitative research for leadership.

This study started from the sales data. UNIQLO's sales sat in one database, segmented by day and by product but never by customer, and the question was what it could show that nobody had looked for.

A critical tool he brought to his role here is a set of strong analytical skills. He has proved himself to be trustworthy and dependable, consistently delivering at high levels.

— Director of Brand Strategy, UNIQLO

Problem § 02

During the summer sale, around a dozen top-50 products were bought together, with 0.90+ correlation across eight stores. Examples included biker shorts, the U heavyweight tee, the Supima tee and men's boxers.

Yet prime floor space went to full-outfit mannequins and products unrelated to the correlated basket, promoting LifeWear outfits that had little to do with what was actually selling.

Approach & methods § 03

First, a lot of cleaning. The sales system was manual and messy, so before any analysis I wrestled it into something a correlation matrix could read: Excel for the wrangling, R for the statistics.

Then I drilled into SS 2021, the season UNIQLO was promoting LifeWear. Across the eight NY metro stores, Saturdays and Sundays were peak sales days, and when I ran correlations across products, around a dozen top-50 products moved together at 0.90+, even where one of them, like men's boxers, was a low-volume item.

The store visit

Data gets you a hypothesis; a floor gets you an explanation. On a 90°F August Saturday afternoon, I went to the SOHO store to see the correlation in the wild.

In one afternoon I watched roughly four hundred transactions unfold, the same volume pattern the dataset had been showing me at the weekly level. Biker shorts and tees were everywhere in the baskets.

I also noticed what was getting in the way: rain jackets and full-outfit mannequins occupying prime floor space, promoting LifeWear staples that had nothing to do with what was actually selling that afternoon.

The why

Back at my desk, I chased the correlation in four directions.

  • Social & adsUNIQLO's own channels weren't pushing these specific items. The demand was coming from somewhere else.
  • WeatherThe biggest sales days followed weeks of 90°F+ highs. Heat was a reliable leading indicator.
  • CultureThe tees had cultural salience I hadn't priced in — Tyler, the Creator tweeted about the Uniqlo tee; GQ wrote it up. On the women's side, Hailey Bieber and Lyn Slater were driving "off-duty biker short" looks, and #uniqlo and #bikershorts tags were thick with the product.
  • PricingA competitive sweep showed our best-selling tees at roughly half the price of competitors — with review scores as good or better. That combination was winning on hot weekend days almost violently. The biker shorts didn't have the same price advantage, but quality plus cultural salience carried them.

Result § 04

Move the data-driven products into key floor positions, and feature them in displays on weekends and hot-weather weeks.

The reasoning is behavioural. It is a salience and choice-architecture intervention: align what shoppers see first with what they are actually choosing, instead of with what the seasonal campaign says they should choose.

What I'd do next § 05

I'd push harder, earlier, for customer-level data. The correlation was the ceiling of what product-only data could tell me: it showed what moved together and hinted at when, but not who was walking in. A single customer identifier would turn a strong hypothesis into a testable segment, and the floor recommendation into a repeatable system.

Tool stack Excel for cleaning and wrangling the manual POS data. R for the correlation matrix and the weekday/weekend decomposition. Notebook and phone camera for the SOHO visit.
The behavioural lens Salience and choice architecture: what shoppers see first shapes what they choose, so the floor should lead with what they already choose.
