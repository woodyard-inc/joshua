Case Study · 002 · 2024

Money can't buy happiness— but community can

InstitutionCity, University of London ProgrammeMSc Behavioural Economics RoleLead researcher & author Duration~6 months · 2024 SupervisorDr Anke Plagnol ResultFirst-class honours · 71%
In short

A cross-cultural analysis of 11,249 respondents across Austria, China, Russia, the United States, and South Africa found that social capital is positively associated with life satisfaction in every country studied — but which dimensions matter, and how much, is moderated by cultural values, corruption, human development, and natural disaster risk. Income, notably, showed no significant main effect.

Wellbeing research often tries to define a "happy life". This dissertation goes at it with a dataset most of the field has overlooked — and finds the answer changes depending on where you live.

The question § 01

Three literatures sit near each other without talking: Putnam's social capital framework, Hofstede's cross-cultural values, and the subjective wellbeing tradition running from Easterlin to Diener. Each topic looks to define the structural conditions, beyond individual wealth or circumstance, that make human life go well.

Studies using the World Values Survey typically measure trust or social networks, rarely both, and almost never the full Putnam triad — networks, trust, norms of reciprocity. The 2017 ISSP Social Networks module had just been released, contained items for all three dimensions, and had barely been touched in the published literature.

The question was whether treating social capital as genuinely multi-dimensional, across genuinely different national contexts, would produce findings the single-dimension studies couldn't see.

Overall, this is a very comprehensive study of the relationship between life satisfaction and social capital in various national contexts. It was a pleasure to read this thoughtful analysis.

— Dr Anke Plagnol · supervisor

Instrument & sample § 02

The dependent variable was life satisfaction, measured on the ISSP's single-item 7-point Likert scale. Social capital was operationalised through nine constructs: trust in society, trust in fairness, organisational trust, social networks (Lin's position generator, weighted by ISEI occupational status), personal and organisational support resources (Van der Gaag & Snijders' resource generator, adapted by the ISSP team), norms of reciprocity (three: elder care, family first, societal care), and contact frequency (daily and organisational). Multi-item constructs were reduced via PCA with reliability checks (most α ≥ 0.71).

Five countries were selected for maximum cross-cultural variance on development, governance, and cultural values: Austria, China, Russia, the United States, South Africa. National-level moderators came from Hofstede's cultural dimensions, HDI, Social Progress Index, Corruption Perception Index, INFORM natural-hazard risk, and within-country income percentiles.

Controls: age, partner status, ISCO-08 skill level, household financial ease. Missing data (3,968 of 15,217 rows had at least one missing value) were handled via MICE with predictive mean matching, imputed within country to preserve national characteristics.

What the data said § 03

Four hypotheses, four findings.

H1a · Within

Social capital is positively associated with life satisfaction within each country.

Supported.

Trust in fairness was significant and positive in all five countries (β = 0.09 to 0.29, p < .01). Organisational trust, likewise. Other dimensions produced country-specific patterns rather than universal effects.

What the data couldn't say § 04

Three honest limits.

The design is cross-sectional, so association is the correct verb throughout — not effect, not impact. Any of the relationships I found could reflect reverse causation (satisfied people invest more in community) or a third variable (personality, which the ISSP doesn't measure).

R² around 0.22 means the model explains roughly a fifth of variance in life satisfaction. That's within the normal range for this literature, but it means four-fifths of what determines whether someone reports being satisfied with their life is not in this model.

The "moderation by income" finding needs a specific caveat. Absolute income showed no significant main effect in the full model — but household financial ease, a subjective measure of making ends meet, was one of the strongest predictors throughout. The Easterlin-style reading ("money doesn't matter") is therefore only partly right: absolute income didn't predict satisfaction, but felt financial strain absolutely did.

Recommendation § 05

For policy-makers and development institutions in already-developed economies, the finding that matters is the interaction between state capacity and social capital. Once a country crosses a threshold of human development, further gains in life satisfaction appear to come more reliably from investments that build trust, fairness, and community participation than from investments that raise GDP.

In high-risk environments — natural disaster, conflict

Community emergency planning and regular civic events are a low-cost wellbeing intervention. Social capital is one of the few things that survives when infrastructure doesn't.

In individualistic cultures

The scarce resource is bridging social capital, not bonding. Subsidise the third places — parks, pubs, employment centres, national sporting events — that create weak ties across groups.

In all contexts

Perceived fairness of institutions is the most consistent cross-cultural predictor, which argues for transparency, judicial quality, and progressive taxation as wellbeing policy, not only economic policy.

Outcome § 06

Submitted as dissertation for MSc Behavioural Economics at City, University of London. Awarded a first-class honours (71%)."

The methodological contribution — treating the underused ISSP 2017 module as a single instrument capable of measuring all three Putnam dimensions simultaneously — is the part I think is most portable to further work.

What I'd change § 07

Three moves I'd make with the project again.

The design. A longitudinal panel — even two waves — would let me separate "social capital leads to satisfaction" from "satisfied people build social capital." Every cross-sectional wellbeing study eventually hits this wall; I'd rather hit it earlier.

The instrument. The ISSP norms items are three questions doing the work of a construct that really wants ten. The norms-of-reciprocity finding was the weakest in the paper, and I think the scale is why — not the phenomenon.

Personality. Tulin, Lancee and Volker (2018) show personality traits predict social capital forms, and the ISSP doesn't measure them. A Big Five module bolted onto a wellbeing survey would tell us how much of what I'm calling national variation is actually personality distributions varying by country.

Tool stack R — mice, lsmeans, emmeans, car for VIF. Excel for early wrangling. The ISSP codebook as gospel.
What surprised me Organisational support resources had a negative association with life satisfaction in the country-level models — the opposite of what I expected. Reading it as "people dislike having to rely on institutions" took me a week.
Quietly proud of Reconstructing the ISCO-08 skill levels for Chinese agricultural workers, where the ISSP module has a known missing-data issue flagged on p. 279 of its own documentation — so the Chinese sample didn't collapse.
The quote on my desk Rousseau, 1750 — "Money can't buy happiness." 274 years later and the data still mostly agrees.
